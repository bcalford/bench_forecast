# Neil M. Gorsuch: Working Profile

## Interpretive method

Gorsuch begins with statutory text and treats it as controlling. He tends to decline to adjust a statute for efficiency or policy reasons. He says the "business of enacting statutory fixes" belongs to Congress (Perry, 2017, dissenting). Where a law is unworkably vague, he would have courts treat it "as a nullity and invite Congress to try again" rather than write a substitute (Davis, 2019). He stresses that "the text is where we must begin (and often end)," and he faults the majority for leading with precedent and assumed purposes (Delligatti, 2025, dissenting). Many of his majority opinions turn on a single word or phrase:

- "any" means "every" (SAS Institute, 2018)
- "money remuneration" (Wisconsin Central, 2018)
- "a notice to appear" (Niz-Chavez, 2021)
- "owed... another" (Henson, 2017)

He reads statutory words as a reasonable reader would have understood them at enactment, using period dictionaries (SFFA, 2023, concurring; Nestlé, 2021, concurring). For constitutional provisions, he invokes "original public meaning" and common-law background (Torres, 2021, dissenting; Timbs, 2019, concurring; Trump v. Barbara, 2026, dissenting). He also looks to common-law defaults when Congress is silent, for example on standards of proof (E.M.D. Sales, 2025, concurring) and on tort duties (586 U.S. 446, 2019, dissenting).

A structural premise appears across his work: courts must stay in their "constitutionally assigned lane" (June Medical, 2020, dissenting). Creating causes of action is "an act of legislation" (Egbert, 2022, concurring in the judgment; Jesner, 2018, concurring).

## Weight given to precedent

He is willing to discard precedents he views as inconsistent with text or history:

- *Auer* (Kisor, 2019)
- *Chevron* (Loper Bright, 2024)
- *Smith* (Fulton, 2021)
- *Quill* (Wayfair, 2018)
- the *Insular Cases* (Vaello Madero, 2022, concurring)
- *Bivens* (Egbert, 2022)
- *Oil States* (Arthrex, 2021)

In Loper Bright he set out his view of stare decisis. At common law, "the opinion of the judge" and "the law" were not "one and the same thing." He criticized retaining *Auer* "only because of stare decisis" while hollowing it out (Kisor, 2019).

He also invokes precedent as a constraint:

- He accused a majority of defying precedent on remedies (Collins, 2021).
- He relied on *Worcester* (Castro-Huerta, 2022) and *Wong Kim Ark* (Trump v. Barbara, 2026).
- He applied existing incorporation doctrine while noting a preferred original vehicle (Timbs, 2019).

## How this justice tends to write separately

He writes separately in 28% of cases (27% recently) and has authored 62 majority opinions (SCDB). His separate writings fall into recurring types:

1. **Short concurrences that join in full but flag open questions** "for another day" (Brnovich, 2021; Talevski, 2023; Jack Daniel's, 2023; Wayfair, 2018).
2. **Concurrences in the judgment on narrower or more fundamental grounds**, such as standing, mootness, or abolishing a doctrine (American Legion, 2019; Muñoz, 2024; Egbert, 2022; Percoco, 2023).
3. **Long historical essays** (Brackeen, 2023; Vaello Madero, 2022; Trump v. Slaughter, 2026).
4. **Dissents urging dismissal as improvidently granted** (Kemp, 2022; Parrish, 2025).
5. **Occasional solo dissents** (Babcock, 2022).

Stylistically, he often opens with:
- a narrative or a second-person hypothetical (Biestek, 2019; Delligatti, 2025; Mallory, 2023)
- rhetorical questions (Trinity Lutheran, 2017)

He frequently signals disagreement with "Respectfully." He also engages colleagues' separate writings directly (Masterpiece, 2018; Learning Resources, 2026).
