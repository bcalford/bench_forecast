# Brett M. Kavanaugh: Working Profile

## Interpretive method

- **Statutory cases start with text.** He says he is "not at liberty to rewrite the statute passed by Congress and signed by the President" (Henry Schein, 2019). He calls "Read the statute" "the most fundamental principle of statutory interpretation" (Becerra v. Empire Health, 2022, dissenting).
- **Traditional tools come before any tiebreaker.** He holds that exhausting the "traditional tools of construction" will "almost always" yield a best reading. He uses that premise to:
  - narrow Auer deference (Kisor, 2019, concurring in the judgment);
  - confine the rule of lenity to "grievous" ambiguity (Shular, 2020, concurring; Wooden, 2022, concurring).
- **Substantive canons get scrutiny.** He asks whether a substantive canon is "sufficiently rooted in constitutional principles or congressional practices" (Rudisill, 2024, concurring).
- **Terms of art and agency practice matter.** He reads legal terms of art by their common-law meaning (Medical Marijuana v. Horn, 2025, dissenting). He treats long-consistent agency practice as interpretive evidence:
  - "45 years of consistent agency practice" (Sackett, 2023, concurring in the judgment);
  - a 30-year Executive Branch reading (Becerra v. San Carlos Apache Tribe, 2024, dissenting);
  - HHS's original reading (Empire Health, 2022).
- **Constitutional method.** He describes it as "text, pre-ratification and post-ratification history, and precedent" (Rahimi, 2024, concurring). He applies a history-and-tradition approach in Establishment Clause cases (American Legion, 2019, concurring) and in Second Amendment cases (Bruen, 2022, concurring).
- **"Who decides?"** He frames many cases this way and assigns policy change to Congress and the President (Bostock, 2020, dissenting; Cummings, 2022, concurring; Arizona v. Navajo Nation, 2023).

## Weight given to precedent

- **He frames precedent as part of Article III "judicial Power."** He invokes Blackstone and Federalist 78 (Ramos, 2020, concurring in part). The excerpt shows only that introduction, not his specific test for overruling.
- **He has joined overrulings.** These include Apodaca (Ramos, 2020) and Roe (Dobbs, 2022, concurring).
- **More often he decides by applying existing precedent:**
  - "This Court's longstanding precedents establish" (Moore v. United States, 2024);
  - Culley (2024);
  - Jones v. Mississippi (2021);
  - Erlinger (2024, dissenting).
- **He narrows precedent through classification rather than overruling it.**

  - He describes what remains of earlier rules (CIC Services, 2021, concurring).

- **Not shown in the excerpts:** NRSC (2026) addresses whether Colorado II remains good law, but the excerpt does not show how he resolved stare decisis there.

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Narrowness.** He repeatedly specifies what a decision does not decide:
  - "what this case is not about" (Nielsen v. Preap, 2019, concurring);
  - "shall-issue" licensing left intact (Bruen, 2022);
  - impact-fee schedules left open (Sheetz, 2024);
  - the cause question undecided (Trump v. Cook, 2026).
- **Counting areas of agreement.** He tallies where Justices agree, such as "all nine Justices" (Bucklew, 2019; Trump v. Vance, 2020; Trump v. J.G.G., 2025). He also minimizes the distance between competing opinions (Kisor, 2019; Lange, 2021).
- **Separation of powers as an interpretive default.** Congress, not courts, creates and extends causes of action (Cummings, 2022).
- **Practical consequences.** He cites real-world effects:
  - victims' recoveries (Purdue, 2024);
  - fiscal costs (San Carlos Apache, 2024);
  - officer danger (Barnes, 2025);
  - crime trends (Davis, 2019).
- **Mens rea as a fair-notice safeguard**, offered in place of lenity (Wooden, 2022; Bondi v. VanDerStok, 2025).
- **Statute-history openings.** Opinions often open with a brief history of the statute ("Congress passed and President … signed"), e.g., Borden (2021) and Seven County (2025).
