# Amy Coney Barrett: Working Profile

## Interpretive method

**Statutory cases.** Barrett's opinions consistently start from ordinary meaning, text, and structure.

- **Text over perceived purpose.** In dissents she accuses the majority of departing from text: "statutes often go further than the problem that inspired them, and under the rules of statutory interpretation, we stick to the text anyway" (Fischer, 2024, dissenting). She objects when the Court "caters to an outlier meaning" (HollyFrontier, 2021, dissenting) or reads a statute "contrary to the text" (San Francisco v. EPA, 2025, dissenting in part).
- **Structural inferences.** She relies on structure, including inferences from omission. In Esteras (2025), she held that Congress's enumeration of factors while omitting one "raises a strong inference" against considering it. She also attends to grammar: in Bartenwerfer (2023), "Written in the passive voice, § 523(a)(2)(A) turns on how the money was obtained."
- **Silence and ratification.** She resists reading statutory silence as conferring rights (Perttu, 2025, dissenting). She rejects thin congressional-ratification inferences drawn from legislative materials (Wooden, 2022, concurring in part; Minerva, 2021, dissenting).
- **Substantive canons.** She distinguishes tie-breaking canons from "strong-form" canons that "strain statutory text." She treats the major questions doctrine as "a tool for discerning—not departing from—the text's most natural interpretation" (Biden v. Nebraska, 2023, concurring). She reaffirmed this against Justice Gorsuch's account and said "less obvious" contextual clues can suffice (Learning Resources, 2026, concurring).

**Constitutional cases.** She writes from an originalist premise.

- **What history counts.** "The history that matters most is the history surrounding the ratification of the text." Postenactment history "requires some justification other than originalism simpliciter" (Rahimi, 2024, concurring).
- **Scrutiny of historical evidence.** She regularly questions how history is used:
  - Late-19th-century evidence is "far too late" (Samia, 2023, concurring in part).
  - The record can be "more silent than supportive" (Fulton, 2021, concurring).
  - She questioned "hunting for historical forebears on a restriction-by-restriction basis" and proposed a standard instead (Vidal, 2024, concurring in part).
  - She identified open questions about liquidation, tradition, and the choice between 1791 and 1868 (Bruen, 2022, concurring).
  - She distinguished history's role at each Bruen step (Wolford, 2026, concurring).

## Weight given to precedent

Barrett frequently decides by applying existing precedent directly.

- **Applying settled lines.** PennEast (2021, dissenting) begins: "A straightforward application of our precedent resolves this case." In Mallory (2023, dissenting) she objected that the majority's approach "does not formally overrule" International Shoe "but it might as well." She concurred in Viking River (2022) solely because "reversal is required under our precedent."
- **Reaffirming existing holdings.** She said Thiboutot "remains good law" (Talevski, 2023). She treated the parental-rights result as "dictated by existing law" (Mirabelli, 2026). She had "no quarrel with Carpenter" (Chatrie, 2026).
- **Containing weak doctrines.** Where she views a doctrine as weakly grounded, she confines it rather than overruling it. Rooker-Feldman "stands on shaky ground, [so] we have consciously kept its footprint small" (T.M., 2026). Sosa's "door" was closed (Cisco, 2026).
- **Reconsidering precedent cautiously.** She has called arguments against Smith "serious" and found its textual and structural critiques "more compelling." She still declined to overrule it without a defined replacement (Fulton, 2021).
- **Precedent within originalism.** She lists stare decisis as one route by which postenactment history can "control the outcome" (Rahimi, 2024).

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Deciding no more than necessary.** This theme appears repeatedly:
  - "I would say nothing more than that" (Viking River, 2022).
  - "I would decide no more than that" (Trump v. Anderson, 2024).
  - "a court of review and not first view" (Biden v. Texas, 2022, dissenting).
  - Remand rather than independent factfinding (Glossip, 2025).
  - Objections to reaching waived or unbriefed issues (Trump v. Cook, 2026; Perttu, 2025).
  - "leave that dispute for another day" (Monsalvo, 2025).
  - Joining the dismissal of a case whose shape had shifted (Moyle, 2024).
- **Limits on inherent and implied judicial power.**
  - She is skeptical of courts of appeals' supervisory power (Tsarnaev, 2022) and of the Supreme Court's own (Hunter, 2026, citing her scholarship).
  - She curtailed universal injunctions (CASA, 2025).
  - She holds that Congress, not courts, creates causes of action (FS Credit, 2026; Cisco, 2026).
- **Precision about standing elements.** She prefers redressability over a broader Article III principle (United States v. Texas, 2023). She warns against "muddy[ing] the waters of standing doctrine" (Gutierrez, 2025). She rests standing on pocketbook injury rather than candidacy (Bost, 2026).
- **Anti-circumvention of constitutional limits.** She objects to states that "manufacture 'consent'" to jurisdiction (Mallory, 2023) and to special exceptions to sovereign immunity lacking "textual, structural, or historical support" (PennEast, 2021).
- **Institutional tone.** "Writings on the Court should turn the national temperature down, not up" (Trump v. Anderson, 2024).
