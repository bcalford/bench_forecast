# Sonia Sotomayor
## Interpretive method

In statutory cases she starts with text but reads it alongside structure, drafting history and stated purpose. She treats canons as tools that do not displace those other sources: "There is no need to seek refuge in a canon of construction" (FAA v. Cooper, 2012, dissenting). She faults readings that isolate "a single phrase" against a statute's "stated purpose" (Adoptive Couple v. Baby Girl, 2013, dissenting). She reads § 903 in light of "the structure of the Code and the language and purpose" (Puerto Rico v. Franklin Cal. Tax-Free Trust, 2016, dissenting). She defends committee reports as "a particularly reliable source" (Digital Realty Trust v. Somers, 2018, concurring).

She also argues from plain text when it is decisive:

- "Case closed" (Luna Torres v. Lynch, 2016, dissenting).
- "The answer is as obvious as it seems: No" (Pereira v. Sessions, 2018, majority).
- She objected that the Court "renders the statutory language... meaningless" (Marx v. General Revenue Corp., 2013, dissenting).
- She called one majority "purportedly textualist" for elevating "piecemeal dictionary definitions" over context (Garland v. Gonzalez, 2022, dissenting in part).

She tests readings against concrete hypotheticals, such as a babysitter suing under Sarbanes-Oxley (Lawson v. FMR, 2014, dissenting) or a waiter's billing becoming identity theft (Dubin v. United States, 2023, majority). Historical equity practice informs remedial statutes (Kappos v. Hyatt, 2012, concurring; Romag Fasteners, 2020, concurring in the judgment).

In constitutional cases she prefers settled doctrinal tests to history-only inquiries. She would follow "tried-and-tested First Amendment precedent" rather than "uncharted" historical analysis (Vidal v. Elster, 2024, concurring in the judgment). Under Bruen she favors a historical inquiry "calibrated to reveal something useful and transferable" (United States v. Rahimi, 2024, concurring).

## Weight given to precedent

She treats stare decisis as binding even where she might decide differently: "Statutory stare decisis compels me to agree" (Kurns, 2012). She endorsed stare decisis in Bay Mills (2014, concurring). She applies precedents she opposed: she continues "to believe that Bruen was wrongly decided" but joined its application (Rahimi, 2024).

She keeps recording disagreement with recent precedents in later cases:

- Daimler's "at home" test "has no home in our precedents" (BNSF, 2017).
- "This Court should not have started down this path" with Trinity Lutheran (Carson, 2022).
- She disagrees with how Sorrell has been read (Janus, 2018).

She frequently charges majorities with departing from precedent:

- The Court "all but overrules" Martinez and Trevino (Shinn, 2022).
- The ruling "cannot be squared with" Seila Law (Collins, 2021).
- It upsets 90 years of reliance on Humphrey's Executor (Trump v. Slaughter, 2026).
- It "Cast[s] aside longstanding precedent" (Mahmoud, 2025).

In Brandt (2014, dissenting) she argued the majority failed to "grapple with" older cases.

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Context-specific standards over categorical rules:** Penn Central balancing (Horne, 2015), Strickland's "all the circumstances" (Dunn v. Reeves, 2021), ERISA's "context-specific inquiry" (Hughes v. Northwestern, 2022), and totality for reasonable suspicion (Glover, 2020).
- **Deciding narrowly and leaving issues open:** She would remand rather than decide in the first instance (Lozman, 2013; BNSF, 2017; Wesby, 2018). She flags open questions (Betterman, 2016; Brownback, 2021; Martin, 2025; U.S. Bank v. Village at Lakeridge, 2018).
- **Practical effects on individuals:** She describes effects on people left without relief (Terry, 2021, concurring; Garland v. Gonzalez, 2022; Grants Pass, 2024).
- **Government burdens and good faith:** She insists on proof of reasonable suspicion (Glover). She stresses prisons' obligations under the PLRA (Ramirez v. Collier, 2022, concurring). She emphasizes finality against official reversals (Cameron, 2022, dissenting) and "the rule of law" (Trump v. J.G.G., 2025, dissenting).
- **Church-state separation:** This is a recurring dissent theme (Trinity Lutheran; Carson; Mahmoud).

## How this justice tends to write separately

She writes separately in 22% of cases, rising to 28% recently, and has written 114 majority opinions, 19 of them in recent terms (SCDB).

**Dissents.** They often open by recasting the majority's framing:
- "To hear the Court tell it" (Trinity Lutheran, 2017).
- "A casual reader... could be forgiven" (Adoptive Couple, 2013).
- "a very reasonable story about a case that is not before us" (Halleck, 2019).

They close with "I respectfully dissent," once "In sadness, I dissent" (Skrmetti, 2025). She also dissents from emergency-docket orders (J.G.G., 2025; Department of Education v. Louisiana, 2024; Allen v. Milligan, 2026).

**Concurrences.** They frequently state a limiting reading, typically introduced as "With that understanding, I join" (Bartenwerfer, 2023; Viking River, 2022; Pung, 2026; CompuCredit, 2012). Other concurrences respond to colleagues' separate writings (Digital Realty, 2018; Bondi v. VanDerStok, 2025; Hamm v. Smith, 2026).

**Concurrences in the judgment.** These usually offer an alternative, often narrower ground (Fernandez, 2026; Vidal, 2024; Barr, 2020).

**Partial dissents.** She splits her vote often, joining some parts of an opinion and dissenting from others (BNSF, 2017; Collins, 2021; Regents, 2020).

**Majority opinions.** They typically state the question and holding in the first paragraph (Hurst, 2016; Pereira, 2018).
