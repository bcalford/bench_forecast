# Samuel A. Alito, Jr.
## Interpretive method

Alito's statutory work starts from text, ordinary usage, and dictionary meaning. He cites Webster's and asks how people actually talk (Muldrow, 2024, concurring in the judgment). He builds everyday hypotheticals such as season-ticket drawings and Olympic doping (Maslenjak, 2017, concurring in the judgment). He traces period definitions back to Coke, Blackstone, and the 1933 Black's (Sekhar, 2013, concurring in the judgment).

He applies the canons but treats them as presumptions, not rules. He relied on noscitur and ejusdem generis in Yates (2015, concurring in the judgment). He warned that the series-qualifier canon is "highly sensitive to context" (Facebook v. Duguid, 2021, concurring in the judgment).

Clear text controls over perceived purpose or sympathy:

- He followed the bump-stock text even though Congress likely saw "no material difference" (Garland v. Cargill, 2024, concurring).
- He rejected a weekend extension of a statutory deadline (Monsalvo Velazquez, 2025, dissenting).

Where text is less determinate, he looks to:

- **Statutory purpose and congressional intent** (Hillman v. Maretta, 2013, concurring in the judgment; Moncrieffe, 2013, dissenting).
- **Practical consequences** (Descamps, 2013, dissenting, urging "a more practical reading"; Rehaif, 2019, dissenting; Esteras, 2025, dissenting).
- **History and tradition** in constitutional questions (Jardines, 2013, dissenting; Nelson v. Colorado, 2017, concurring in the judgment; Pung, 2026, majority).

In 2014 he described an agency as free to resolve statutory ambiguity under Chevron (Scialabba, 2014, dissenting).

## Positions by area of law

- *Habeas and AEDPA:* He stresses finality (Christeson, 2015; Banister, 2020, both dissenting). He also wrote a capital habeas ruling for the petitioner on investigative funding (Ayestas, 2018, majority).
- *Federal criminal statutes:* He often favors the broader government reading (Descamps, 2013; Johnson, 2015; United States v. Taylor, 2022, all dissenting; Brown, 2024, majority).
- *Votes for defendants:* He has voted for defendants on narrow statutory grounds (Yates, 2015; Sekhar, 2013; Maslenjak, 2017) and on a breadth-based First Amendment ground (Packingham, 2017). All four were concurrences in the judgment.

## Weight given to precedent

He treats stare decisis as a doctrine that must be applied consistently.

- He faulted the Court for overruling Harris "with barely a mention of stare decisis," then named Apprendi as a better candidate for reconsideration (Alleyne, 2013, dissenting).
- He objected when majorities discarded recently reaffirmed rulings (Johnson, 2015, dissenting) or long-settled lower-court interpretations (Rehaif, 2019, dissenting).

He has also argued for overruling precedents he views as non-interpretive or unworkable:

- Brulotte was "a bald act of policymaking" (Kimble, 2015, dissenting).
- Abood was overruled on the grounds of its reasoning, workability, consistency with other cases, later developments, and reliance (Janus, 2018, majority).

Where a majority will not revisit a line of cases, he applies it:

- He applied Raich even while noting he has questions elsewhere (Taylor, 2016, majority).
- He declined to single out one statute under the existing nondelegation test (Gundy, 2019).
- He invited future reconsideration of Basic (Amgen, 2013, concurring).

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Clear guidance for lower courts.** He criticizes standards that leave judges "to guess" (Elonis, 2015; County of Maui, 2020; Muldrow, 2024; Esteras, 2025). He sometimes supplies lists of permissible rules himself (Reed v. Town of Gilbert, 2015, concurring).
- **Deciding no more than necessary.** He objects to "undisciplined dicta" (Packingham, 2017) and "nonbinding dicta" (Moody, 2024). He declines unnecessary questions (Atlantic Richfield, 2020; United States v. Apel, 2014).
- **Neutral procedural rules and the Court's own rules.** Examples include res judicata and issue preclusion (Whole Woman's Health, 2016; 587 U.S. 329, 2019), questions presented (Madison v. Alabama, 2019), grounds not raised below (Bondi v. Vanderstok, 2025), and full briefing before summary reversal (Christeson, 2015; Lombardo, 2021).
- **Detailed factual records.** He often opens dissents by restating the facts, including crime facts (Johnson, 2015; Gutierrez, 2025; Thornell, 2024, majority). He insists on the "real case" (584 U.S. 414, 2018, dissenting).
- **Law-enforcement and practical stakes** (Williams, 2012; Mitchell, 2019; Moncrieffe, 2013).

## How this justice tends to write separately

He writes separately in about 22% of cases, essentially unchanged recently at 21% (SCDB). His separate writings take several recurring forms:

- **Pointed dissent openings.** Examples are "The Court is tired of the Armed Career Criminal Act" (Johnson, 2015), the ACA "trilogy" (California v. Texas, 2021), the Homeric "chimera" (Thompson v. Clark, 2022), and "Exhibit A" (Esteras, 2025). These dissents often use rhetorical questions and roadside hypotheticals (Walker, 2015) and numbered lists of the majority's errors (Gutierrez, 2025).
- **Concurrences in the judgment.** He agrees with the outcome but rejects the majority's reasoning, sometimes explicitly calling the opinion "unhelpful" (Muldrow, 2024; Moody, 2024; Geo Group, 2026).
- **"On the understanding" concurrences.** These narrow the holding to its minimum (United States v. Cooley, 2021; Kennedy v. Bremerton, 2022; Ciminelli, 2023; McElrath, 2024).
- **Forward-looking concurrences.** These flag issues for remand or later cases (Amgen, 2013; Acevedo Feliciano, 2020; Mallory, 2023).
- **Brief join-notes.** These record limited disagreement with an opinion he otherwise joins (Brumfield, 2015).
- **Text-compelled concurrences.** He explains that the text compels a result and calls on Congress to amend the statute (Cargill, 2024; Sackett, 2012).
