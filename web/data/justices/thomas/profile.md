# Clarence Thomas: Working Profile

## Interpretive method

Thomas consistently grounds constitutional analysis in original public meaning and historical practice. He invokes "the original meaning of the Sixth Amendment" (Alleyne, 2013, opinion of Thomas, J.) and would define "Officers of the United States" by the term's founding-era meaning (Lucia, 2018, concurring). He calls for re-examining doctrines the Court "has never purported to ground" in original understanding (Murr, 2017, dissenting). He rejects "modern public opinion" and "evolving standards of decency" as interpretive metrics (Grants Pass, 2024, concurring).

When text runs out, he turns to common-law and founding-era practice:
- the law of tenders, to decide mootness (Campbell-Ewald, 2016, concurring in judgment);
- the remedies available in the 1789 English Court of Chancery, to define "equitable relief" (Liu, 2020, dissenting);
- the history of the Marian bail and committal statutes, to define the Confrontation Clause (Ohio v. Clark, 2015, concurring in judgment).

In statutory cases he prefers text and structure. He declines to rely on implied pre-emption when the statute alone resolves the case (Hughes, 2016, concurring in part). He decides treaty cases "principally on the plain meaning of the treaty's text" (Monasky, 2020, concurring in part). He reads terms in context, even against common-law meaning, where that meaning "does not fit" (Husky, 2016, dissenting). Separation-of-powers analysis starts from the exclusivity of the three Vesting Clauses (Dep't of Transp. v. AAR, 2015, concurring in judgment; Seila Law, 2020, concurring in part).

## Weight given to precedent

Thomas will urge overruling precedent he views as "demonstrably erroneous" (Jones v. Mississippi, 2021, concurring in judgment). He has called for overruling:
- Grutter (Fisher, 2016);
- Hibbs (Coleman, 2012);
- Humphrey's Executor (Seila Law, 2020);
- Atkins (Hamm v. Smith, 2026);
- Robinson (Grants Pass, 2024).

He authored majorities overruling Harris (Alleyne, 2013) and Nevada v. Hall (587 U.S. 230, 2019).

Even so, he regularly joins opinions applying precedent "which no party has asked us to reconsider" (Byrd, 2018; Murr, 2017). He also joins where "our precedents dictate that holding" (United States v. Bryant, 2016, concurring). Where he doubts a line of cases, he would "limit [it] to the precise circumstances" (Perry, 2012). He openly revisits doctrines he once joined (Sineneng-Smith, 2020; Vaello Madero, 2022).

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Repeated statements of settled views.** Many opinions restate a fixed position on a single issue: "I continue to adhere" on Confrontation (Smith v. Arizona, 2024) and the FAA in state courts (Kindred, 2017).
- **Jury factfinding and the categorical approach.** He objects to judicial factfinding that raises sentences and to the categorical approach itself (Taylor, 2022; Sessions v. Dimaya, 2018, dissenting).
- **Doubts about judge-made doctrines.** He questions vagueness doctrine (Dimaya, 2018), overbreadth (Sineneng-Smith, 2020), Katz (Carpenter, 2018), and implied causes of action (Egbert, 2022, majority).
- **Spending legislation as contract.** He treats spending-power legislation as a contractual offer rather than law that secures rights (Talevski, 2023).
- **Alternative constitutional grounds.** He looks to the Privileges or Immunities Clause (Murr, 2017) and the Citizenship Clause (Vaello Madero, 2022) as other textual footholds.
- **Party presentation.** He enforces strict party presentation and record limits (Ross v. Blake, 2016; Unicolors, 2022).
- **Equity limited to 1789.** He confines equitable remedies to founding-era chancery practice (Liu, 2020).

## How this justice tends to write separately

He writes separately in 28% of cases over his career and 36% recently (SCDB). His separate writings take several recurring forms:

- **Full joins with a broader agenda.** He often joins the majority "in full" but writes to flag a broader doctrinal problem and invite future challenges. Examples include "the Court, and lower courts, should revisit" (Hemani, 2026), "we should reassess" (Medina, 2025), and "I would welcome briefing" (Byrd, 2018).
- **Concurrences in the judgment on a different rationale.** Examples include Descamps (2013), Campbell-Ewald (2016), and Chiafalo (2020).
- **Brief dissents restating a standing position.** These can run only a paragraph (DIRECTV, 2015).
- **Partial joins.** He excludes sections he considers unnecessary or not properly presented (Matal, 2017; Monasky, 2020).
- **Recommendations to dismiss.** Rather than decide ancillary issues, he would dismiss as improvidently granted (Unicolors, 2022; Medical Marijuana, 2025).
- **Factual openings in criminal dissents.** His dissents in criminal cases often begin with a detailed account of the offense (Lynch, 2016; Taylor, 2022; Hamm v. Smith, 2026).
