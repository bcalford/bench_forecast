# John G. Roberts, Jr.
## Interpretive method

Roberts's statutory opinions start from the enacted text and treat departures from it as decisive errors. He rejects results that rest on "a requirement found nowhere in the text of the statute" (T-Mobile South v. Roswell, 2015, dissenting). He declines inquiries that are "sensible as [they] may be" but are "not the one Congress adopted" (Paroline, 2014, dissenting). He relies on a "straightforward reading of the statute's text" and on statutory structure (Ysleta del Sur Pueblo, 2022, dissenting; Salazar v. Ramah Navajo, 2012, dissenting). He reads provisions as a "harmonious whole" and does not treat internal tension as ambiguity (Scialabba, 2014, concurring in the judgment).

In constitutional cases he works from text, structure, history and precedent together:

- He collected the Constitution's seventeen uses of "Legislature" (Arizona State Legislature, 2015, dissenting).
- He relied on 19th-century treatises defining "elements" (Alleyne, 2013, dissenting).
- He traced the Framers' experience with legislatures acting as courts (Patchak, 2018, dissenting).
- In Eighth Amendment cases he insists on "objective indicia" of state practice rather than judicial or clinical judgment (Miller, 2012, dissenting; Moore v. Texas, 2017, dissenting).
- He resists functionalism in structural cases (Wellness v. Sharif, 2015, dissenting).

A recurring premise is that courts decide law, not policy. "This Court is not a legislature" (Obergefell, 2015, dissenting). "Our role… is to apply the law, not to answer such questions" (Miller, 2012, dissenting). "We do not consider whether the Act embodies sound policies" (NFIB v. Sebelius, 2012).

## Weight given to precedent

Roberts gives stare decisis substantial weight, including for decisions he thinks wrong.
- He agreed Bellas Hess was wrongly decided but would not overrule it. He stressed that departures demand "special justification," a burden that is higher where Congress can act (Wayfair, 2018, dissenting).
- He followed Whole Woman's Health despite having dissented from it, because stare decisis requires treating "like cases alike" (June Medical, 2020).
- He applied Moore (2017) after dissenting from it (Moore v. Texas, 2019, concurring).
- He declined to overrule Auer (Kisor, 2019, concurring in part).
- Earlier, he would await a properly briefed case before reconsidering Auer (Decker, 2013, concurring).

He does vote to overrule:
- He wrote the opinion overruling Williamson County's state-litigation requirement (Knick, 2019).
- He agreed Roe and Casey's viability line should be discarded "under a straightforward stare decisis analysis," while opposing full overruling (Dobbs, 2022).

He also reads precedents narrowly to avoid extending them (Wilkinson, 2024, dissenting; Sessions v. Dimaya, 2018, dissenting).

## Signature doctrines and recurring themes

- **Deciding no more than necessary.** "If it is not necessary to decide more to dispose of a case, then it is necessary not to decide more" (Dobbs, 2022). See also his preference for narrow grounds (Wellness, 2015), his concurring only in the judgment when analysis is "beside the point" (Kebodeaux, 2013), and his leaving room for exceptions so as not to "embarrass the future" (Florence, 2012, concurring).
- **Article III as structural limit and safeguard.** He opposes legislation deciding particular cases (Bank Markazi, 2016, dissenting; Patchak, 2018, dissenting). He opposes consent to Article III violations (Wellness, 2015). He opposes advisory opinions through nominal damages or moot claims (Uzuegbunam, 2021; Campbell-Ewald, 2016).
- **Limits on agency and executive authority.**
  - Courts, not agencies, decide the scope of delegated power (Arlington, 2013, dissenting).
  - Chevron "is not a license for an agency to repair a statute" (Scialabba, 2014).
  - He objected to presidential defiance of a statute (Zivotofsky v. Kerry, 2015, dissenting) and to an end-run around notice-and-comment rules (Arizona v. San Francisco, 2022, concurring).
  - He found executive construction lacking "express authority of Congress" likely unlawful (National Park Service, 2026, dissenting).
- **Practical workability and reliance.** He worries about remedies (Upper Skagit, 2018, concurring), uninjured class members (Tyson Foods, 2016, concurring), settlement incentives (Actavis, 2013), and guidance to officers (569 U.S. 141, 2013).
- **State sovereignty and equal treatment of States** (Shelby County, 2013; Trevino, 2013).

## How this justice tends to write separately

Roberts rarely writes separately: 8% of cases career and 3% recently. He is in the majority 96% of the time recently and has authored 19 majority opinions in that period (SCDB).

His separate writings fall into recognizable types:

- **Short concurrences confining a holding.** He joins the Court while marking its limits (Caniglia, 2021; Erlinger, 2024; Florence, 2012), or explains his own vote in a sentence or two (Wilkinson, 2024; Kansas v. Nebraska, 2015).
- **Concurrences in the judgment on narrower grounds** (Dobbs, 2022; Kebodeaux, 2013; Lange, 2021; Scialabba, 2014).
- **Bridging opinions** that narrow the distance between factions (Kisor, 2019).
- **Dissents conceding the policy appeal or fairness of the result** while rejecting its legal basis:
  - "That seems fair. But… the word 'fair' does not appear" (Franchise Tax Bd. v. Hyatt, 2016).
  - "Amy deserves restitution" (Paroline, 2014).
  - "This bottom-line conclusion does not trouble me" (Murr, 2017).
- **Vivid framing devices.** His dissents often open with hypotheticals or pointed rhetoric (Bank Markazi, 2016; Alleyne, 2013; Lange, 2021; Arizona State Legislature, 2015; 576 U.S. 186, 2015).
